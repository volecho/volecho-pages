THERA captures the beautiful mess of being everywhere, all at once

Live albums have always carried a certain risk. Take the music out of the studio and the safety net disappears. A vocal might crack. The crowd might drown out a lyric. A song performed perfectly on record can take on a completely different personality under arena lights. For THERA, those imperfections are precisely the point.

At just 19, the Czech singer-songwriter has already spent enough time on major stages to understand that a live performance cannot be recreated once the night is over. Her new seven-track EP, "everywhere, all at once," does not attempt to polish those moments into something cleaner than they were. Instead, it preserves them as they happened, with each song taken from a different stop on her first arena tour.

Together, the recordings recreate the setlist that travelled from venue to venue, but the result is more than a document of a tour. It feels like a collection of emotional snapshots. Different rooms. Different crowds. Different nights. One artist growing more comfortable with the size of the stage beneath her feet.

That sense of movement sits at the center of "everywhere, all at once." The title itself captures the strange emotional state of touring, where every day brings a new city while the performer is still carrying the emotional residue of the previous night. Airports, soundchecks, hotel rooms, arenas, applause, silence, then another city. Life becomes fragmented, yet the music provides a thread connecting everything.

THERA has chosen not to hide that instability.

The EP embraces the small differences between performances, allowing the atmosphere of each venue to become part of the recording. Rather than presenting a carefully corrected version of a concert, the songs retain the energy that only exists when an artist and audience are sharing the same room. You can feel the difference between performing a song and experiencing it in real time.

It is a decision that brings "everywhere, all at once" closer to classic live records than the heavily edited concert albums that often sound suspiciously similar to their studio counterparts. Think of Fleetwood Mac's "The Dance," where old songs found new emotional lives years later, or Nirvana's "MTV Unplugged in New York," a performance that became more important because it sounded human and vulnerable. THERA is working in a much more contemporary pop setting, but the same principle applies. The room matters.

The standout moment comes with "rewind," recorded during the final show at Prague's O2 Arena. The song already carries emotional weight, but the circumstances surrounding this performance give it another dimension. This was not simply another date on the schedule. It was the closing night of THERA's European tour supporting Jason Derulo, and it happened in front of a home audience at the largest arena in the Czech Republic.

For a young artist, that combination could easily become overwhelming. Family and friends were in the crowd. The pressure of performing at home was amplified by the knowledge that this particular night marked the end of a major chapter. But those conditions seem to have pushed the performance somewhere more emotionally exposed.

The closing moments of "rewind" capture that release. There is gratitude in the performance, but also the strange sadness that comes when something important ends. Touring often looks glamorous from the outside, but the end of a tour can feel unexpectedly quiet. After weeks of movement, constant noise, and thousands of faces, the lights go down and suddenly there is nowhere else to be.

That emotional contrast gives "rewind" its power.

The EP also includes THERA's version of Charli XCX's "Break the Rules," a choice that makes perfect sense within a live setting. The original carries an attitude built for communal release, and THERA's rendition became a crowd favorite during the tour. Covers can sometimes feel like filler on a setlist, but this one appears to serve another purpose. It creates a bridge between artist and audience, a moment of instant recognition before returning to THERA's own material.

Charli XCX is an interesting point of reference for THERA beyond the cover itself. Both artists understand the tension at the heart of modern pop, where a song can be built for movement while carrying something more complicated underneath. THERA has described the direction of her forthcoming second album as a mixture of dance music and sad pop, a combination that has become increasingly important in recent years.

That territory has produced some of the strongest pop music of the past decade. Robyn made a career out of turning heartbreak into dance-floor release. Lorde's "Melodrama" found drama in the space between the party and the morning after. Carly Rae Jepsen has repeatedly shown how bright melodies can carry complicated feelings, while artists such as Rina Sawayama and Charli XCX have pushed pop toward stranger and more emotionally unpredictable places.

THERA's interest in combining dance tracks with sadness suggests she understands the appeal of contradiction.

After all, some of the best songs to dance to are not happy songs.

There is something almost therapeutic about that combination. The body is moving while the mind is processing something else entirely. Anyone who has ever danced to a song about heartbreak understands the strange logic of it. Music can allow two emotions to exist at once without forcing either one to win.

"everywhere, all at once" may be a live EP, but it also works as a useful marker of where THERA currently stands. She is young enough to still be considered an emerging artist, yet her recent history already includes stages that many performers spend years hoping to reach.

Her rise began with "guardian angel," a track that connected strongly with online audiences and helped introduce her to listeners outside her immediate circle. The song gathered millions of views on TikTok and crossed the million-stream mark on Spotify, leading to appearances on major editorial playlists.


The momentum continued when THERA became the first Czech ambassador for Spotify's Global EQUAL campaign, a moment that placed her image in New York's Times Square. For an artist from the Czech Republic, it was a highly visible signal of how quickly her audience was expanding beyond national borders.

Her debut album, "I Wish I Called," then reached number one on the Czech iTunes chart, further establishing her as an artist capable of translating online attention into a more lasting audience.

But streaming statistics and billboard appearances only tell part of the story.

Live performance is where artists are truly tested.

A recording can be replayed endlessly until every detail is right. A stage offers no such luxury. Every note exists once. If something unexpected happens, it becomes part of the night. THERA's decision to release an EP built around those conditions suggests an artist who understands the value of that risk.

Supporting Jason Derulo across Europe placed her in front of audiences who did not necessarily arrive knowing her songs. That is one of the hardest environments for any support act. You have limited time to make an impression, and the crowd is waiting for someone else.

She has faced similar challenges opening for Loreen in London, Manchester, Dublin, and Zurich, as well as supporting Remember Monday. Each audience comes with different expectations, and each city responds differently.

The experience seems to have strengthened THERA's ability to connect quickly.

That is what makes "everywhere, all at once" more interesting than a standard collection of concert recordings. It documents an artist learning in public. The performances are not presented as perfect monuments. They are moments taken from a period of rapid growth.

The best live music often contains evidence of risk.

Listen to Queen at Live Aid and part of the excitement comes from knowing that nothing can be edited afterward. Watch Amy Winehouse's performances and the smallest changes in phrasing can completely alter the emotional meaning of a familiar song. Even modern arena pop, with all its choreography and technology, still depends on the unpredictable exchange between artist and audience.

THERA seems particularly interested in preserving that exchange.

There is a cinematic quality to the EP's concept as well. Seven songs taken from different cities create something similar to a coming-of-age film told through fragments. Prague is one scene. Another city becomes the next. The same character appears throughout, but the surroundings keep changing.

It brings to mind films like "Lost in Translation," where movement through unfamiliar spaces creates an emotional distance from ordinary life, or Richard Linklater's "Before Sunrise," where a limited period of time becomes more meaningful because everyone involved knows it cannot last forever. The scale is obviously different, but the emotional logic is familiar. Temporary places can leave permanent marks.

Touring is built on that same contradiction.

You can spend one night performing in front of thousands of people, feeling completely connected to the room, and wake up the next morning in a city where nobody knows who you are. Then the cycle begins again.

The title "everywhere, all at once" captures that feeling remarkably well.

It also feels appropriate for an artist whose career has accelerated quickly. At 19, THERA has already released a debut album, reached major streaming milestones, performed internationally, appeared on some of Europe's biggest stages, and begun work on a second album.

That amount of activity could easily create pressure to keep moving faster. But the live EP suggests a brief pause, an opportunity to preserve what has already happened before rushing toward the next release.

There is wisdom in that.

The music industry has become obsessed with constant output. Artists are expected to remain visible at all times, feeding audiences with singles, clips, updates, and announcements. A live EP like this offers something different. It looks backward for a moment.

Not nostalgically, but honestly.

It says these nights happened. These songs sounded different here. This crowd responded this way. This was the final show. This is what it felt like.

That sense of documentation gives the record its emotional center.

THERA's next chapter may take her toward a more dance-focused sound, but "everywhere, all at once" makes a strong case for holding onto the unpredictability that live performance brings. The studio can shape a perfect version of a song. A stage reveals what remains when perfection is no longer the goal.

For listeners drawn to emotionally direct pop, this EP offers an interesting entry point into THERA's world. Fans of Lorde, Griff, Holly Humberstone, Maisie Peters, and the more reflective side of Charli XCX may find something to connect with here. Those artists share an understanding that pop music can be immediate without being emotionally empty.

The next album will likely reveal more about where THERA wants to take that idea. The combination of dance rhythms and sad pop has plenty of possibilities, especially for an artist who already seems comfortable balancing intimacy with larger stages.

For now, "everywhere, all at once" captures a specific moment before it disappears.

That is ultimately what makes live recordings valuable. They cannot truly be repeated. You can perform the same song again, use the same arrangement, stand under the same lights, but the night itself is gone.

THERA understands that.

Across these seven tracks, she has collected pieces of different cities and placed them beside one another. The result is not a perfect representation of a tour because no recording could ever be that. Instead, it feels like something more personal, a collection of evidence from a period when life was moving quickly and music was the one constant.

At the center is "rewind," appropriately named for a song that captures the end of something while preserving it for another listen.
